## Introduction

Most of the mathematics you have seen thus far runs forwards. Given initial conditions, solve a differential equation; given a mass distribution, compute its gravitational field; given a function, compute its integral.

An inverse problem runs the other way. Instead of starting with a system and predicting what we would observe, we start with observations and try to reconstruct the underlying system, its properties, or the processes that produced them. In other words, rather than asking what effects a given cause produces, we ask what can be inferred about the cause from its effects.

## Inverse problems

The notion of an *inverse problem* is vague and difficult to define. However, we can build a mental model of what we mean by an inverse problem. First, let us consider a (forward) problem: we have some given *data* and we seek some unkown *solution*. In practice, solving a problem means that we take the known data, perform some mathematics on it, and arrive at a solution. An inverse problem reverse this process: we have some solution and we seek the corresponding data.

$$
\boxed{\text{Data}}
\underset{\text{Inverse problem}}{\overset{\text{Forward problem}}{\rightleftarrows}}
\boxed{\text{Solution}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Data}}
\underset{\text{Inverse problem}}
{\overset{\text{Forward problem}}{\mathrel{\mathop{\longleftrightarrow}}}}
\boxed{\text{Solution}}
$$

In 1902, Jacques Hadamard proposed three criteria that a well-behaved mathematical problem should satisfy. A problem is said to be *well-posed* if it satisfies all of the following three criteria:

1. *Existence:* There exists a solution.
2. *Uniqueness:* There is at most one solution.
3. *Stability:* The solution depends continuously on the data.

A problem that fails to satisfy one or more of these criteria is called *ill-posed*.

Inverse problems are frequently ill-posed: solutions may not exist, may not be unique, or may depend very sensitively on the observations. Hadamard himself regarded well-posedness as an essential requirement for problems arising in physics, viewing ill-posed problems as largely artificial mathematical constructions. The development of inverse-problem theory would eventually demonstrate just how important such problems are in practice.

## A first example: differentiation

Consider the following forward problem: integration. Let $A$ be the forward operator which takes a continuous real-valued function $f \colon [a, b] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ to its integral such that

$$
A(f(x)) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) dt \qquad a \leq x \leq b.
$$

We can model $A$ as an operator between spaces such that $A \colon C([a, b]) \rightarrow C([a, b]),$ where $C([a, b])$ is the collection of all continuous real-valued functions $[a, b] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$.

Its associated inverse problem is the following. Given the accumulated integral $A(f(x))$, can we determine the original function $f$? That is, we want to invert the operator $A$: given some $g$, we need to find some $f$ such that $Af = g$. In order to do this, we will need the *Fundamental Theorem of Calculus*.

:::theorem{title="Fundamental Theorem of Calculus - Part 1"}

Let $f \colon [a, b] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be continuous. Let $F$ be the function defined for all $x \in [a, b]$ by

$$
\tag{1}
F(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) dt.
$$

Then $F$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$ and $F'(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in (a, b)$.

:::

In particular the inverse problem appears to have a very simple solution, namely, differentiation:

$$
\boxed{f(x) = F'(x)}
$$

Let us now see if this inverse problem is well-posed according to Hadamard's three criteria listed above.

1. *Existence:* Does a solution exist?

That is, given $m(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) dt$, does there exist $f$ such that $m(x) = f(x)$? By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus we have that $\int_{a}^{x} f(t) dt = \int_{a}^{x} m'(t) dt = m(x) - m(a)$. Thus if $m(a) = 0$, then a solution exists.

2. *Uniqueness:* Is the solution unique?

Suppose that we have two continuous functions $f_1$ and $f_2$ solving the inverse problem. Then $\int_{a}^{x} f_1(t) dt = \int_{a}^{x} f_2(t) dt$. Differentiating both sides and applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus yields $f_1(x) = f_2(x)$. Thus, there cannot be two different solutions producing the same data. The solution is unique.
