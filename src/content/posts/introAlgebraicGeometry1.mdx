## Introduction

*Algebraic geometry* is a branch of mathematics that combines techniques of (commutative) algebra with the language and the problems of geometry.

## Bézout’s Theorem

Let us discuss a classical result known as *Bézout’s Theorem* on intersection of curves. The phenomenon we try to investigate is the total number of intersection points of curves in the plane. For now, by a *curve* we will mean the graph of a polynomial $f(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]$ in the variables $x$ and $y$.

Let us observe the following. Two straight lines (degree $1$ curves) intersect in one point. A line (degree $1$) and a quadratic curve (degree $2$) intersect in two points. Two quadratic curves (degree $2$) intersect in four points. A quadratic curve (degree $2$) and a cubic curve (degree $3$) intersect in six points. We arrive at the following conjecture which Newton, MacLaurin and Euler regarded as self-evident.

:::conjecture

If $f(x, y)$ is a polynomial of degree $d$ and $g(x, y)$ is a polynomial of degree $e$, then the curves $f = 0$ and $g = 0$ intersect in $d \cdot e$ points.

:::

## Summary

:::theorem{title="Extreme Value Theorem"}

Every continuous function on a compact space is bounded.

:::

:::lemma{title="Boundedness of Convergent Sequences"}

Every convergent sequence is bounded.

:::

:::corollary{title="Boundedness"}

Every continuous function on a compact metric space is bounded.

:::

:::proposition{title="Uniqueness of Limits"}

A convergent sequence in a metric space has a unique limit.

:::

:::definition{title="Metric Space"}

A metric space is a set equipped with a distance function.

:::

:::example{title="The Real Numbers"}

The real numbers with the usual distance form a metric space.

:::

:::remark{title="Completeness"}

Not every metric space is complete.

:::
