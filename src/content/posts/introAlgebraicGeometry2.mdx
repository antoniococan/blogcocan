## Introduction

In [Introduction to Algebraic Geometry - 1](https://antoniococan.github.io/blogcocan/posts/introalgebraicgeometry1/), I motivated the study of algebraic geometry using Bézout’s Theorem on the number of intersection points of plane curves. Roughly speaking, Bézout’s Theorem states that two curves defined by polynomials $f(x, y) = 0$ and $g(x, y) = 0$ having degrees $d$ and $e$, respectively, intersect in exactly $d \cdot e$ points.

Many problems arising in algebraic geometry are not limited to the common zeros of polynomials in two variables nor to solving two equations. We can thus consider a system

$$
\tag{1}
	\begin{cases}
		f_{1}(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}) = 0 \\
		f_{2}(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}) = 0 \\
		\vdots \\
		f_{r}(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}) = 0.
	\end{cases}
$$

For instance, we may intersect surfaces, hypersurfaces, etc. We are thus going to build the theory around the common zero sets of polynomials in $n$ variables.

## Affine Algebraic Sets

We first define an ambient space in which our desired geometric objects can be defined. Let $k$ be a field (e.g. $\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}, \mathbb{Z} / \langle p \rangle, \ldots$).

:::definition{title="Affine space"}

We define *affine* $n$*-space over* $k$ by

$$
\mathbb{A}^{n}_{k} := \{ a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n) : a_i \in k \}.
$$

Elements $a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ of $\mathbb{A}^{n}_{k}$ are called *points* and $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ are called *(affine) coordinates* of the point $a$.

:::

Now that we have our ambient space defined, we define the objects that live within this space, namely geometric objects that are cut out by the zeros of polynomial equations.

:::definition{title="Affine algebraic sets"}

Let $S \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ be a set of polynomials. We define the *zero set* (or *vanishing set*) $\mathbb{V}(S)$ of $S$ by

$$
\mathbb{V}(S) := \{ a \in \mathbb{A}^{n}_{k} : f(a) = 0, \forall f \in S \}.
$$

A subset $X \subseteq \mathbb{A}^{n}_{k}$ is called an *affine algebraic set* if $X = \mathbb{V}(S)$ for some $S \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$.

:::

:::remark

Observe that we motivated the definition of an affine algebraic set by solving a finite system of polynomials as in (1). However, in Definition 2, the set $S$ of polynomials may contain infinitely many polynomials. Thus a priori we are not solving a finite system. However, it turns out that every such set $S$ will be *generated* by finitely many polynomials and the zero set of these finitely many generators will agree. This remarkable fact follows from Hilbert's Basis Theorem which we discuss below.

:::

The following proposition summarizes how affine algebraic sets behave under set-theoretic operations. The first statement says that if the set of polynomials is larger, the set
of common zeros will be smaller. The second statement asserts that an arbitrary intersection of affine varieties is again an affine variety. The last statement says that a finite union of affine varieties is an affine variety

:::proposition

1. If $S_1 \subseteq S_2$, then $\mathbb{V}(S_1) \supseteq \mathbb{V}(S_2)$.
2. For subsets $S_i \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ we have that

$$
\bigcap_{i \in I} \mathbb{V}(S_i) = \mathbb{V} \Big ( \bigcup_{i \in I} S_i \Big ).
$$

3. For any two subsets $S_1, S_2 \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ we have that

$$
\mathbb{V}(S_1) \cap \mathbb{V}(S_2) = \mathbb{V}(S_1 \cdot S_2),
$$

where the product $S_1 \cdot S_2$ is defined by

$$
S_1 \cdot S_2 := \{ f \cdot g : f \in S_1, g \in S_2 \}.
$$

:::

:::proof

Exercise. □

:::

Let us observe that different collections of polynomials can define exactly the same affine algebraic set. For instance, $S_1 = \{ x, y \}$ and $S_2 = \{ x^2, xy, y^2 \}$ both have the same zero set

$$
\mathbb{V}(S_1) = \{ (0, 0) \} = \mathbb{V}(S_2).
$$

This is not merely a coincidence. We can enlarge a collection of polynomials considerably without changing the affine algebraic set it defines. A natural question then is: Given a collection of polynomials $S$, what is the largest collection of polynomials $\langle S \rangle$ so that $\mathbb{V}(S) = \mathbb{V}(\langle S \rangle)$?

## Noetherian Rings and Hilbert's Basis Theorem

:::definition{title="Noetherian rings"}

A ring $A$ is called *Noetherian* if all of its ideals are finitely generated, that is, for any ideal $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A$ there are finitely many generators $f_1, \ldots, f_r \in A$ so that

$$
\mathfrak{a} = \langle f_1, \ldots, f_r \rangle.
$$

:::

An important equivalent way to define Noetherian rings which is usually taken as the definition is the following *finiteness condition*.

:::proposition{title="Characterization of Noetherian rings"}

Let $A$ be a ring. Then the following two conditions are equivalent.

(i). $A$ is Noetherian.

(ii). $A$ satisfies the *ascending chain condition* (a.c.c.) for ideals, meaning that, each increasing chain of ideals

$$
\mathfrak{a_1} \subset \mathfrak{a}_2 \subset \ldots
$$

must eventually terminate, that is, there exists some $m$ such that $\mathfrak{a}_{m} = \mathfrak{a}_{m + 1} = \ldots .$

:::

:::proof

$(i) \Rightarrow (ii)$ 

$(ii) \Rightarrow (i)$ □

:::

:::theorem{title="Hilbert's Basis Theorem"}

If the ring $A$ is Noetherian, then $A[x]$ is Noetherian. By induction we then have that $A[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is Noetherian.

:::

:::proof

We prove the contrapositive, that is, we prove that if $A[x]$ is not Noetherian, then $A$ is not Noetherian. Suppose that $A[x]$ is not Noetherian. Then there exists some ideal $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A[x]$ which is not finitely generated.  □

:::

:::remark

Hilbert's Basis Theorem is named after the German mathematician David Hilbert. The word *basis* in the theorem comes from the old terminology that a basis for an ideal is a finite generating set. Thus in this terminology the statement of the theorem is that every ideal of $A[x]$ has a basis, where $A$ is assumed to be a Noetherian ring.

:::
